IT3081 Statistical Modelling · Group Project
Credit Card Customer Churn Analysis

Project Overview

Customer Retention Decision-Support Dashboard

Consultancy Prototype. This dashboard demonstrates how statistical findings from a credit card customer churn analysis could support a banking team's customer-review process. All records are anonymised demonstration data from the BankChurners dataset. No production banking system is represented.
Executive Summary

This report investigates recorded credit-card attrition using 10,127 BankChurners customer records. Descriptive and inferential results identify substantial differences in transaction activity and variation across customer groups. These associations guide investigation but do not establish causes of departure.


Among the evaluated models, targeted spline logistic regression provided the strongest cross-validated probability performance. In the internal modelling holdout, it achieved ROC-AUC 0.9599 and identified 259 of 326 attrited records at the training-selected threshold, while incorrectly flagging 101 existing records.


An academic Customer Retention Decision Support Dashboard prototype has been developed to demonstrate the proposed review process. Written feedback from an experienced card-centre banking professional (21 years’ experience) supports the practical relevance of the proposal while prioritizing inactivity, reduced spending and validation against operational data.


Tasks 6–9 critically evaluate future methods; no experiment, PCA model, Bayesian analysis or time-series model is claimed as completed. Operational integration, dated-data validation and a controlled retention pilot remain future work.

Business Question
How can a retention team use customer activity patterns, model scores and available staff capacity to decide which records to review — and then evaluate whether its actions are useful?
Proposed Workflow
1. Examine customer activity patterns from historical data
2. Score records using a validated statistical model
3. Prioritise records for human review based on scores and team capacity
4. Record review decisions and proposed actions
5. Evaluate outcomes through a controlled pilot

Proposed Controlled Pilot Evaluation
Before operational deployment, a controlled evaluation would establish whether the proposed review process provides value beyond current practice.
1. Verify Dated Organizational Data
Confirm that the organization possesses timestamped transaction records and departure dates that allow prospective model evaluation — not just retrospective label reconstruction.
2. Independent Model Validation
Retrain and validate the model on recent organizational data. Compare calibration and discrimination against the results observed in this prototype.
3. Define Review & Contact Policy
Establish what the retention team will actually do when reviewing flagged records. This policy must exist before measuring whether review improves retention.
4. Run a Controlled Pilot (RCBD or CRD)
Randomly assign flagged customers to review (treatment) or standard service (control). A Randomized Complete Block Design, blocking on score tier, would control for score-related confounding. A Completely Randomized Design is simpler but may be sufficient if sample size is adequate.
5. Evaluate Retention, Cost, Workload & Experience
Measure retention rates in both groups after a defined follow-up period. Assess cost per retained customer, analyst workload, and customer experience outcomes. A single metric (accuracy) is insufficient.
6. Revise the Process Based on Evidence
Use pilot results to decide whether to scale, modify, or discontinue the review process. Document lessons for future iterations.

Modelling Approach Summary
Key methodological decisions and their justification

Model family: Logistic regression with natural spline basis expansion for variables exhibiting non-linear log-odds relationships (identified via Likelihood Ratio Test, p < 0.001).


Selection criterion: Training 10-fold cross-validated log loss — a proper scoring rule that rewards accurate probability estimates rather than just correct classifications.


Threshold selection: Maximum F1 score on training out-of-fold predictions (0.3392). This balances precision and recall without using test data for selection.


Competitors evaluated: Baseline logistic regression, Ridge, LASSO, and Elastic Net (α = 0.25, 0.50, 0.75). All used the same 10-fold cross-validation partition for comparability.


Why not higher complexity: The spline logistic model achieves strong discrimination (AUC 0.96) while remaining interpretable. The improvement over penalised models justifies the additional complexity. More complex methods (random forests, gradient boosting) were not considered because the assignment focuses on statistical modelling techniques.

Critical Evaluation: Principal Component Analysis (Task 7)
Assessment of whether dimensionality reduction would benefit this analysis

Conclusion: PCA is not recommended for this dataset.


The BankChurners dataset contains 19 predictor variables — a moderate dimensionality that does not require reduction for computational reasons. The Spearman correlation matrix (Task 3) identifies only 3 strong correlations, suggesting limited redundancy among predictors.


Potential advantages: PCA could reduce multicollinearity between correlated pairs (e.g., Credit_Limit and Avg_Open_To_Buy, r = 0.99). It could also compress the predictor space when combined with penalised regression.


Limitations for this problem: PCA components are linear combinations that sacrifice individual variable interpretability. In a retention context, managers need to understand which customer behaviours are associated with attrition — not that "Component 3" is elevated. Additionally, PCA assumes linear relationships; the non-linear patterns captured by our spline model would be lost if PCA were applied first.


When PCA should be considered: Datasets with hundreds of correlated features (e.g., genomics, text-derived features), problems where interpretability is secondary to prediction, or as a preprocessing step for methods sensitive to multicollinearity.

Critical Evaluation: Bayesian Statistical Methods (Task 8)
Assessment of Bayesian approaches for this business problem

Naïve Bayes: Would provide a computationally efficient baseline classifier. However, its conditional independence assumption is violated by the correlated transaction variables in this dataset. The BankChurners dataset already includes pre-computed Naïve Bayes scores (removed during preprocessing), suggesting this method has been considered by the data provider.


Bayesian Logistic Regression: Would allow incorporating prior information about expected attrition rates and coefficient magnitudes. With weakly informative priors (e.g., Student-t with 3–7 degrees of freedom), Bayesian logistic regression would provide full posterior distributions for each coefficient, quantifying parameter uncertainty more naturally than confidence intervals. This is particularly valuable when the organization has domain knowledge about expected relationships.


Bayesian Decision Making: A formal Bayesian decision framework could incorporate the costs of false positives (unnecessary review effort) and false negatives (missed attrition) to derive an optimal decision threshold. This would replace the current F1-based threshold with one that reflects actual organizational costs — a significant improvement for operational deployment.


Recommendation: Bayesian approaches are appropriate and could improve the analysis, particularly for threshold selection and uncertainty quantification. They were not implemented in this project due to scope constraints, but represent a promising direction for a pilot evaluation.

Critical Evaluation: Time Series Analysis (Task 9)
Assessment of time series methods for customer retention

Current limitation: The BankChurners dataset provides only cross-sectional aggregates (e.g., total transaction count, total amount) — not dated monthly or weekly records. Without timestamped data, traditional time series methods (ARIMA, exponential smoothing, seasonal decomposition) cannot be applied.


If dated data were available:


• Trend analysis: Monthly transaction counts per customer could reveal declining engagement patterns before departure.

• Seasonal analysis: Spending patterns may vary by month (e.g., holiday seasons, salary cycles), and adjusting for seasonality could improve attrition prediction.

• ARIMA modelling: Autoregressive models could forecast individual customer activity trajectories, flagging accounts with predicted engagement declines.

• Aggregate forecasting: Portfolio-level attrition rates could be forecast using ARIMA or exponential smoothing, supporting workforce planning and budget allocation.


Expected organizational benefits: Early warning systems based on engagement trajectory changes, proactive rather than retrospective identification, and seasonal staffing for retention teams.


Recommendation: The organization should invest in collecting and storing timestamped transaction records to enable time series approaches in future iterations of this analysis.

Known Limitations & Assumptions
Constraints that affect interpretation and operational applicability
Dataset limitations:
  • Retrospective labels — we observe who left, not who will leave
  • No dated transaction history — only aggregated totals
  • Unknown sampling frame — selection criteria for inclusion are undocumented
  • Unknown data vintage — the period these records represent is unspecified
  • Unverified currency context — income categories use dollar signs ($), but specific national currency (e.g. USD) is unverified; financial values represent "supplied monetary units"
  • "Unknown" categories in Education (15%) and Income (11%) may indicate systematic missing data

Modelling assumptions:
  • Independent observations — customer behaviour may be influenced by shared events
  • Logistic regression assumes a specific functional form (log-odds linear in transformed predictors)
  • Training-test split assumes rows are exchangeable — violated if there is temporal structure
  • The threshold was optimised for F1 balance — not for a specific organisational cost structure

What this prototype does not claim:
  • That flagged customers will respond to retention offers
  • That the model produces validated future-departure probabilities
  • That the dashboard has saved customers or money
  • That the prototype represents a partner bank's system
Strategic Direction. Proceed with a staged, human-reviewed retention decision-support programme rather than immediate operational deployment. Statistical models identify high-risk associations but do not establish causality or customer responsiveness. Management should sponsor a bounded validation and pilot programme with explicit decision gates.
1. Define Engagement Metrics
Operational definitions beyond binary attrition

• Agree separate, explicit definitions for account closure, inactivity, reduced spending, and activation/reactivation, including designated observation and outcome windows.


• Reflect the expert's emphasis on declining transaction frequency and spend contraction as precursors to departure.


• Avoid arbitrary automated actions (e.g., automatic closure after 60 days of inactivity) without contextual investigation.

2. Longitudinal Data Infrastructure
Extracting dated records for prospective assessment

• Secure authorized extraction of dated transactions, payment histories (12–24 months as recommended by industry feedback), and supplementary card data.


• Enforce strict time-ordered evaluation: fix the decision date, strictly exclude post-decision information from model predictors, and evaluate outcomes prospectively.


• Transition from cross-sectional static snapshots to time-series behavioral tracking (Task 9).

3. Validate Leading Spline Model
Local calibration & trade-off evaluation

• Retain the targeted spline logistic model as the leading benchmark (test AUC: 0.9599, recall: 79.5% at threshold 0.3392).


• Conduct independent validation on current operational bank data to assess calibration, discrimination, and subgroup stability before acting on scores.


• Acknowledge operational trade-offs: the internal test identified 259 attrited customers but generated 101 false positives that require staff review effort.

4. Capacity-Based Review Sizing
Human-in-the-loop workload & outreach controls

• The illustrative 17.8% flag rate (360 / 2,026 test records) is an exploration threshold, not an operational outreach quota.


• Align review queue volumes strictly with staff capacity, cost-benefit thresholds, and customer contact preferences.


• Empower analysts to review context (e.g., secondary card usage, service tickets) before initiating approved phone calls or SMS outreach.

Controlled Pilot & Organizational Decision Criteria
Rigorous experimental validation prior to institutional rollout
Phase A: Shadow Operations (30–60 Days)
Run the decision-support system in shadow mode without customer contact to evaluate data feed stability, score calibration, analyst workload, and decision agreement.
Phase B: Controlled Randomized Pilot (RCBD / CRD)
Randomize flagged customers to retention action vs standard service. Utilize a Randomized Complete Block Design (blocking on predicted risk tier) to control for baseline differences.
Phase C: Multi-Dimensional Outcome Measurement
Measure incremental retention, card activation rates, and spend generated (expert priorities), alongside customer complaints, staff handling time, and net financial contribution after retention costs.
Risk, Ethics & Governance
Responsible data handling and decision safeguards

• Human-in-the-loop: Model scores support human decisions; no automated negative actions or credit reductions should occur solely from scores.


• Demographic fairness: Statistical differences by gender (Task 4) or demographic categories must not be used for discriminatory targeting or disparate service delivery.


• Confidentiality & compliance: Maintain role-based access, audit trails of review notes, and adherence to bank compliance policies.

Future Analytical Enhancements
Extending statistical methodologies (Tasks 7–9)

• Bayesian Decision Framework (Task 8): Formulate loss functions incorporating monetary costs of false positives vs false negatives to set optimal decision thresholds.


• Time Series Modeling (Task 9): Apply ARIMA and trajectory forecasting once timestamped longitudinal records are collected.


• Dimensionality Reduction (Task 7): Re-evaluate PCA only if the predictor space expands into high-dimensional transaction embeddings.
